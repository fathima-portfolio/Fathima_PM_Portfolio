Independent case study, not affiliated with Porter

Porter: Building first-time goods transport confidence

Research, personas, RICE prioritisation, PRD, metrics and rollout plan

Context and role
Independent capstone from a product management program. I was the sole author: research, synthesis, prioritisation, PRD, metrics and rollout plan.
Problem
First-time individual users struggle to turn "my sofa and fridge" into a vehicle choice and a final price. Target: raise successful first-trip completion from 41% to 58% within six months. The 41% baseline and the funnel are reconstructed from survey and interview self-reports, not Porter analytics, and the revenue example uses placeholder volumes.
Evidence
20 survey responses, 3 recorded interviews and app-review mining (23 interactions, directional rather than statistically definitive). Total price was the top concern (11 of 20), then extra charges (10 of 20), vehicle choice and fit (9 of 20) and driver reliability (6 of 20). Two personas: the Anxious First-Mover and the Control-Seeking Switcher.
Decision and trade-offs
Scored 12 ideas with RICE. Top two: a goods-based vehicle recommender (7.65) and a transparent all-in fare (6.56). I chose guidance over raw speed for first-timers, kept the pricing engine out of scope, and flagged driver reliability as the phase-2 candidate.
Plan and metrics
PRD scope for the MVP, six tracked events, and a four-cohort rollout (10%, 25%, 50%, 100%). North Star: successful first-trip completion. Guardrails: cancellations, rebooking after cancel, fare disputes and damage claims.
Risks I planned for
Wrong-size recommendations (bias conservative, easy upsize), slowing repeat users (skippable, off by default for them), a higher all-in fare scaring users (A/B test the framing), and cold start (rule-based first).
Prototype and outcome
A clickable rule-based recommender, "What Vehicle Do I Need?": goods input, vehicle recommendation with confidence and fit rationale, all-in fare, confirm and book. It runs in the browser with no backend and no real Porter pricing. This is a case study, not a launched product.

Loop EV Charging: improving the charging start journey

Context and role
Drawn from my work as iOS lead on an EV-charging app, with product, design and backend. No confidential screenshots, code, customer data or metrics.
Problem
Users could not tell whether a charger was available, whether the app could connect to it, or whether charging had actually started. Each failure created support requests or abandoned attempts.
Decision
A "Ready to Charge" journey: availability, charger details, connection readiness, start charging, charging confirmed. Ask for Bluetooth permission at the moment of need, not at launch. P0: make charging start reliable and observable. P1: permission and connection guidance. P2: discovery and repeat shortcuts.
Success metrics
Start-charging conversion, time from charger selection to confirmed charging, Bluetooth connection success rate, charging-start failure and support-contact rate, permission-denial recovery rate.
Outcome
A proposal and prioritisation, not a shipped result.

Independent product metrics exercise

YouTube: defining product success

Context
An independent exercise in choosing metrics for a product I do not work on.
Problem
Raw watch time can rise through low-quality or addictive content, so it is a risky single goal.
Decision
North Star: meaningful watch sessions per active user. Supporting metrics: 7-day and 28-day retention, and creator success (satisfied supply rate).
Guardrails
Negative feedback and "not interested" signals, policy violation rates, recommendation diversity and creator concentration, session abandonment.
Experiment example
Test a ranking improvement on a defined cohort. Primary: meaningful watch sessions. Secondary: retention and satisfaction. Stop if guardrails regress.
